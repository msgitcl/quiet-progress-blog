Slow Is Not Stopped

02 September 2026

6 min read

You are moving slower than you planned. The timeline you wrote in January has quietly become fiction, and the gap between the plan and the reality has started to feel like evidence about you rather than about the plan.

Fine. Slow is a speed. Stopped is a different thing entirely, and the two get confused constantly because from inside a slow month they feel identical.

Direction outranks speed, and the maths is simple

Pilots use a rule of thumb called the one-in-sixty rule: for every degree you are off course, you will be about one mile off track after sixty miles flown.

It is a small-angle approximation, and it is useful precisely because it makes an abstract idea concrete. One degree is nothing. You cannot feel one degree. Over sixty miles it costs you a mile. Over three thousand miles it puts you roughly fifty miles from where you meant to be — a different city, in a country the size of most.

Now note what the rule does not contain: speed. Flying faster does not reduce the error. It brings you to the wrong place sooner. The only variable that determines where you land is heading.

Speed decides when you arrive. Direction decides where. Almost everyone optimises the first and never checks the second.

The two failures look nothing alike

Being slow on the right heading is recoverable at any point. The distance covered still counts, the skills still accumulate, and picking up pace later multiplies work that has already gone in the correct direction. A slow year on the right road is a year of real progress with a disappointing number attached.

Being fast on the wrong heading is the expensive failure, and it is the one that gets praised while it is happening. It looks like productivity. It produces visible output, busy weeks and a sense of momentum. Then at some point you look up, discover you are a long way from where you intended, and every mile of that speed has to be undone.

This is why "am I going fast enough" is nearly always the wrong question to be worrying about, and "is this still the right direction" is nearly always the one that has not been asked in months.

Why slow feels like stopped

The confusion has a simple cause: your sense of progress is calibrated to changes you can perceive, and real progress at low speed produces almost no perceptible day-to-day change.

Add the fact that the plan created an expectation, and the gap between expectation and reality registers emotionally as decline. Nothing is going backwards. You are simply arriving later than a number you invented in a more optimistic month.

And that number deserves less respect than it gets. It was set before you knew what the work actually involved, by a version of you with incomplete information and a good mood. Missing it is not a failure of discipline; it is the normal outcome of estimating something you had not yet done.

Keeping the direction

If heading is what matters, then the important discipline is not pushing harder — it is checking the heading on a schedule, deliberately, rather than only when something goes wrong.

The check is short and slightly uncomfortable. Where am I actually trying to end up? Is the work I did this month the kind of work that gets me there? Is there anything I am doing purely out of momentum, which made sense a year ago and no longer does?

Done once a quarter, this takes twenty minutes and prevents the years-long version of the one-degree error. Skipped, it is how people end up with a decade of competent work in a direction they never actually chose.

Speed comes back on its own

The last piece is the most reassuring, and it is worth knowing while you are in the slow part.

Speed is largely a function of accumulated skill, established systems and reduced friction — all of which are built by the slow work rather than by pushing. The person who has been at something for two years moves faster than they did at the start, not because they are trying harder, but because the decisions that used to take an hour now take a minute.

So the slow months are not a delay before the productive period. They are the productive period, in its unrecognisable early form. The pace picks up later, on its own, provided the heading was right and you were still on the road.

What to do with this

  • Check heading quarterly, in writing. Where am I going, is this work taking me there, what am I doing purely from momentum.
  • Stop grading yourself against the January estimate. Revise the timeline to what the last three months actually showed. That number is real; the old one was a guess.
  • Distinguish slow from stalled. Slow means it moved a little. Stalled means it did not move at all. Only one of those needs an intervention.
  • Do not add speed to fix doubt. When something feels wrong, working harder in the same direction increases the error. Check the heading first.
  • Measure distance, not pace. Two years in, the question that matters is how far you have come, not how quickly you were moving in March.

You are moving slower than you planned. That is a fact about a forecast, not about your capacity.

Slow is not stopped. Keep your direction — the speed will come.
