Stop Asking If You Are Good Enough. Ask If You Are Willing.

02 September 2026

QPQuiet Progress02 September 2026 · 6 min read

6 min read

"Am I good enough at this?" is the question people spend years quietly circling, and it is close to useless. It cannot be answered in advance, it changes the moment you begin, and whichever answer you settle on tends to become self-fulfilling.

There is a better question, and it can actually be answered today: am I willing?

The ten thousand hours you were told about

The number everyone knows comes from research by Anders Ericsson and colleagues on elite violinists at a music academy in Berlin. The best performers had accumulated large amounts of solitary, effortful, feedback-driven practice — what Ericsson called deliberate practice — and by around age twenty the top group averaged in the region of ten thousand hours.

Two things then happened to that finding. It became a threshold ("do ten thousand hours and you will be expert"), and it became a guarantee. Neither was in the original work. Ten thousand was an average for one group in one domain, not a requirement, and Ericsson spent years afterwards objecting to how the number was being used.

The other correction is less comfortable, and it should be stated honestly rather than skipped. In 2014 Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald published a meta-analysis in Psychological Science asking how much of the variation in performance deliberate practice actually explains. The answers varied a great deal by domain: about 26 percent of the variance in games, 21 percent in music, 18 percent in sports, 4 percent in education, and less than 1 percent in professions.

So practice matters enormously in some domains and much less in others — and in none of them is it the whole story. Starting age, circumstances, resources, opportunity and individual differences all contribute.

What that actually implies

People take one of two conclusions from that meta-analysis, and both are wrong.

The first is "so talent decides everything, and effort is theatre." That does not follow. A quarter of the variance in a competitive domain is an enormous amount, and it is the portion you can act on. Nobody has ever improved by using the unexplained variance.

The second is "so I need to find my natural domain." Also unsupported. What the numbers describe is how much practice explains at the population level, not whether it will work for you in the thing you have chosen.

The honest reading is narrower and more useful. Talent influences how quickly you improve. It does not determine how far you go, because how far depends on how long you keep going — and duration is the variable most people never seriously test, because they stop long before it becomes the limiting factor.

Talent decides how fast. Willingness decides how far. Over any long horizon, far wins.

Willingness is a real thing to assess

Unlike "good enough," willingness can be examined honestly, and the questions are concrete.

Are you willing to be bad at this in front of people for a while? Willing to do the unglamorous part — the repetitions, the corrections, the tedious middle? Willing to keep going through a stretch where nothing improves visibly? Willing to be corrected by someone who knows more, without treating it as an insult?

Those are answerable now, and they predict much more than any assessment of your current ability. Someone with modest aptitude and high willingness beats someone with high aptitude and low willingness in almost every field, given enough time — because the second person leaves.

It is worth noticing what the low percentages in some domains actually mean in practice. Where practice explains little of the variance, it is usually because the field is crowded with people who all practise similarly, so practice stops distinguishing anyone — not because it stopped working. Among people who barely practise at all, the difference it makes is enormous. Almost nobody reading this is at the elite margin where the meta-analysis bites; most of us are in the range where showing up regularly is still the dominant variable by a distance.

Ability is not fixed at the start

There is a further problem with the ability question: you are asking it at the point where you have the least information and the lowest skill.

Almost everyone is bad at a thing for longer than they expected. If you interpret early badness as a verdict on your ceiling, you will quit during a phase that literally everyone passes through. The people who look talented now were, in nearly every case, unimpressive at the start too — you simply were not watching.

A more accurate question is: after a real attempt — say six months of consistent work — what does the trajectory look like? Not the level. The slope. That question takes six months to answer and is worth vastly more than any prediction you can make today.

What to do with this

  • Replace the ability question with the willingness one. "Am I willing to do the boring part for a year?" is answerable, and it is the one that decides the outcome.
  • Judge slope, not level. Six months in, ask whether you are better than you were, not whether you are good.
  • Take the practice seriously anyway. Effortful, corrected, slightly-too-hard practice is where the improvable share sits. Comfortable repetition is not the same thing.
  • Do not use the meta-analysis as an excuse. The unexplained variance is not yours to spend. The practice portion is.
  • Set the horizon in years. "Far" is only available to people who are still there, and duration is the one input entirely under your control.

Stop asking whether you are good enough. You cannot know yet, and the answer would change the moment you started working. Ask the question that is actually load-bearing.

Talent decides how fast. Willingness decides how far. Far always wins.
