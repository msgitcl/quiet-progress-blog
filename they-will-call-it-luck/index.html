They Will Call It Luck. They Did Not See the Hours.

02 September 2026

QPQuiet Progress02 September 2026 · 6 min read

6 min read

When something finally works, a particular word tends to arrive: lucky. Good timing. Right place, right moment. It is usually said warmly, and it quietly deletes several years of your life.

The interesting question is not whether luck is real — it plainly is. It is why the same people keep encountering it.

Luck has behaviour attached to it

The psychologist Richard Wiseman spent years studying people who described themselves as consistently lucky or consistently unlucky, and found the two groups behaved differently in ways that were, in principle, learnable.

The self-described lucky people were more open to unplanned encounters and new experiences, kept broader and looser social networks, were more relaxed and therefore more observant, and were markedly better at recovering from bad outcomes and continuing.

In one well-known demonstration, participants were given a newspaper and asked to count the photographs inside. Early in the paper was a large message stating that there was no need to keep counting — there were forty-three photographs. People who considered themselves unlucky tended to plough on counting and miss it. Those who considered themselves lucky were more likely to notice.

The uncomfortable implication is not that unlucky people are at fault. It is that a portion of what we call luck is opportunity detection: the same event occurs, and one person registers it as an opening while the other, head down and anxious, does not.

Chance is genuinely random. Which chances you are standing near, and whether you notice them, is not.

What the word actually deletes

When someone calls your result luck, run through what they did not see.

They did not see the hours, spread across years, most of them unremarkable. They did not see the notebooks, the deleted drafts, the versions that were worse. They did not see the earlier attempts that failed quietly, each one teaching you what not to repeat. They did not see the state you were in when it was not working.

They saw the moment things became visible, and reasoned backwards from it. From that vantage point, "lucky" is a perfectly sensible inference. It is simply drawn from the small fraction of the record they had access to.

This is worth understanding for one practical reason: you are the only person with the complete record. If you start believing the outside account — that it was mostly chance — you will draw the wrong conclusions about what to do next, and you will be strangely fragile afterwards, because chance is not something you can deliberately repeat.

The part you can actually increase

Take the honest version: outcomes are a mixture of preparation, exposure and chance. You cannot control chance. You can control the other two, and they determine how many chances get near you at all.

Preparation is being ready when something arrives. The opportunity that finds you unable to deliver is not an opportunity; it is a near miss that you will remember. Most of the boring work is preparation for a moment whose timing you do not get to choose.

Exposure is how many chances come past in the first place. Publishing rather than filing. Telling one more person what you are working on. Finishing things and letting them exist in public. Each finished, visible piece of work is a surface that luck can land on, and most people have very few of them.

Neither of these guarantees anything. Both change the number of opportunities you get, which over a long enough period is nearly indistinguishable from being lucky.

None of this should be read as a claim that everyone gets the same chances. They do not, and the differences are large and often structural — money, health, where you were born, who you already know. The argument is narrower: within whatever range of opportunity you actually have, the number of chances that reach you is not fixed. Two people in identical circumstances can end up with very different opportunity counts, and the gap between them is made of finished work and maintained relationships.

Let them call it luck

There is a temptation to correct people. It is not worth it, and there is a better reason than modesty.

Arguing about how hard you worked puts you in the position of defending the result rather than continuing the work. Meanwhile, the account that matters is the one you hold privately: what you actually did, what worked, what you would repeat. That record is your method, and it is the thing you will use again.

Let the outside description be whatever it is. Keep the accurate one, because you are the only person who can.

What to do with this

  • Increase your surface area. More finished, visible work is the most direct way to raise the number of chances that come near you.
  • Be ready before the moment. The unglamorous preparation exists precisely so that the opportunity you cannot schedule does not go past unusable.
  • Keep loose connections alive. Wiseman's lucky people had broad, low-intensity networks. Opportunities arrive disproportionately through people you do not speak to often.
  • Look up occasionally. Head-down execution is necessary and it is also how you miss the message on page one. Schedule time to notice what is going on around the work.
  • Keep your own record. Write down what you actually did. When someone calls it luck, you will still know what it was made of.

Some of it was luck. Most results have some. But luck kept finding you because you kept being in places where it could, doing work that made you worth finding.

Luck is what they call the part they missed.
