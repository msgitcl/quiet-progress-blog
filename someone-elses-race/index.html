Comparison

You Are Not Losing. You Are Watching Someone Else's Race.

02 September 2026

QPQuiet Progress02 September 2026 · 7 min read

7 min read

There is a particular kind of tiredness that has nothing to do with effort. You did fine today. You did the work, you kept your word to yourself, and then you picked up your phone at 11 p.m. and within ninety seconds you felt like you were losing.

Nothing about your day changed in those ninety seconds. Only the measuring stick changed.

Comparison is a measuring instrument, not an emotion

In 1954 the psychologist Leon Festinger published a paper proposing something that now sounds obvious and was not: people have a drive to evaluate their own abilities and opinions, and when there is no objective standard available, they evaluate themselves against other people.

That is the important half of the theory. Comparison switches on when the objective yardstick is missing. Nobody compares their body temperature to a stranger's, because a thermometer exists. But "am I doing enough with my life" has no thermometer. So the mind reaches for the nearest available instrument, which is other people.

Festinger also noticed that we do not compare with just anyone. We select people we judge to be roughly similar to ourselves — same field, same age, same starting point. The closer someone is to you, the more information their result seems to carry about you. This is why a billionaire does not bother you and a person from your own graduating class does.

So comparison is not a character flaw and it is not vanity. It is a measuring instrument that switches on automatically whenever you cannot measure yourself directly. The instrument is not the problem. What it is pointed at is the problem.

The feed removes the denominator

Every statistic has a numerator and a denominator: how many succeeded, out of how many tried. A feed shows you the numerator only.

You see the person who published the book. You do not see the several hundred people in the same position who wrote for two years and stopped. You see the studio-lit result at month eighteen; you do not see months one through seventeen, which looked exactly like your months one through seventeen — dull, slow, unphotogenic, and privately embarrassing.

This is not deception on their part. It is the shape of the medium. Nobody films the boring part, because the boring part is boring. But the effect on you is real: you end up comparing your full unedited life to a sample that has been filtered twice — once by who succeeded, and again by which minutes were worth posting.

You are not comparing yourself to a person. You are comparing yourself to a highlight reel of a highlight reel, with the failures deleted and the denominator missing.

Their chapter twenty is not your chapter one

The second distortion is time. When you meet someone's work, you meet it at whatever stage it happens to be in. There is no timestamp on the feed telling you this is year nine.

Somebody who has been at it for a decade is not a superior version of you. They are a person who is further along a road you are also on, and who spent years being exactly as unimpressive as you feel right now. If you could see their chapter one, most of the sting would go out of it immediately. You cannot, so it stays.

The practical damage is that the comparison does not just make you feel bad — it changes what you do next. It makes ambitious people quit reasonable plans. You had a plan that would have worked in three years, you saw someone at year eight, decided three years was too slow, went looking for a faster method, found none, and stopped. The plan was fine. The reference point ruined it.

The one comparison that actually works

The fix is not "stop comparing." That instruction has never worked for anyone, because the instrument is automatic. The fix is to give it something better to measure.

There is one comparison that is genuinely informative: you, against you, twelve months ago. It uses the same starting conditions, the same constraints, the same body, the same circumstances. It is the only comparison in your life with a controlled variable.

It also has the advantage of being verifiable. You can check it. You cannot check whether a stranger online is actually doing well; you have no access to their finances, their health, their relationships, or how they feel on a Wednesday afternoon. You have complete access to your own record.

Most people who do this honestly are surprised. Progress is nearly invisible from the inside, because you are always standing at the current level and the current level always feels normal. Looking back a year is the only way to see the slope.

What to do with this

  • Notice when the instrument switches on. The urge to compare spikes when you have no direct evidence about how you are doing — late at night, between projects, after a flat week. That is the moment to go and find the evidence instead of scrolling for it.
  • Compare inputs, not outcomes. Outcomes depend on timing, luck, money and years you cannot see. Inputs — did I show up, how many times this month — are yours, visible, and countable.
  • Add the missing timestamp. When something impressive stops you, ask: how long has this person been doing this? Usually the honest answer is "much longer than me," and the comparison quietly collapses.
  • Keep a boring record. A one-line log per day. Its only job is to exist twelve months from now, so that your comparison has real data instead of a mood.
  • Prune the accounts that are pure numerator. Not out of resentment. You are simply removing a badly calibrated instrument from your desk.

You are not losing. You are watching someone else's race from the side of a track you are still running on, at a point in their race you were never shown the start of.

Look down at your own lane. It is the only one that has anything to do with you. And run.
