The Fear Does Not Leave. You Get Better at Moving Anyway.

02 September 2026

6 min read

There is a quiet assumption sitting underneath a lot of stalled plans: that at some point the fear lifts, and that the lifting is the signal to go. Confident people, in this model, are people who stopped being afraid.

Ask anyone who does frightening things routinely and they will tell you the fear is still there. What changed is their relationship to it.

The signal is the same either way

Fear and excitement produce a very similar physical state. Raised heart rate, faster breathing, alertness, tension, a stomach that has opinions. The body is preparing for something significant. What it is not doing is telling you whether that something is good or bad — that interpretation is added afterwards, by you.

Alison Wood Brooks tested this directly in research published in 2014 in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, under the title "Get excited." Participants faced genuinely anxiety-inducing tasks — singing in front of others, public speaking, a difficult maths test — and were instructed to say either "I am excited" or "I am calm," or nothing at all.

The people who reappraised their state as excitement performed better than those who tried to calm down.

The explanation is mechanical rather than mystical. Trying to become calm means moving from a high-arousal state to a low-arousal one, which is a large physiological shift and mostly does not work under pressure. Reframing the same arousal as excitement keeps the physical state exactly where it is and changes only the label — a much shorter distance, and one that shifts you from a threat framing towards an opportunity framing.

The fear does not need to leave. It needs to be read correctly.

Courage is a description of behaviour

The second correction is about what the word actually means.

Courage is not the absence of fear. It cannot be, because an action carrying no risk and no fear requires no courage — it is just an action. Courage is defined by fear being present and the person proceeding anyway. The fear is not the obstacle to bravery; it is the precondition for it.

This matters practically, because if you believe courage is a feeling you have to acquire first, you will keep waiting for a state that never comes and reading its absence as evidence that you are not the kind of person who does this. If courage is a description of behaviour, the requirement changes completely: you do not need a different internal state. You need to move while the current one is running.

What the fear is actually made of

Most fear about ordinary risks — publishing, asking, starting, speaking — is not fear of the event. It is fear of what the event implies about you if it goes badly.

That is why the imagined version is so much worse than the real one. Real failures are specific and bounded: the piece was ignored, the answer was no, the talk went flat. Imagined failures are unbounded, because they are not about outcomes at all — they are about permanent conclusions regarding your worth, and there is no floor to that.

Naming the actual worst case usually shrinks it substantially. Not because the worst case is pleasant, but because a real worst case has edges: it costs a defined amount, lasts a defined time, and is survivable in a way the vague version never is.

There is a useful test for this. Ask whether the thing you are afraid of would still frighten you if nobody ever found out how it went. For most ordinary risks the answer is no, or much less — which tells you the fear was never really about the task. It was about being seen failing at it, and that is a different problem with a different solution: do the first attempts privately, or do them somewhere the audience does not matter to you, and let the skill arrive before the exposure does.

Moving while it is still there

The practical skill is not eliminating fear. It is developing tolerance for acting inside it — and that tolerance is built the only way tolerances are built, through exposure at survivable doses.

Do the small frightening thing. Then a slightly larger one. Each completed attempt adds a piece of evidence that the feeling can be present without dictating what happens next, and it is that evidence, not any argument, that eventually makes the larger things possible.

People who look fearless have almost always simply done this for years. Their fear response is not smaller than yours in any measurable sense. It has just stopped being the thing that decides.

What to do with this

  • Relabel rather than suppress. "I am excited" is closer to the truth than "I am calm," and it is a shorter distance from where your body already is.
  • Write down the real worst case. Specifically: what it costs, who notices, how long it lasts. Vague fear has no ceiling; a written one does.
  • Reduce the dose, not the fear. A smaller version of the frightening thing still produces the evidence you need, at a price you can pay this week.
  • Move before the deliberation starts. The window between deciding and acting is where the fear does its work. Shorten it.
  • Stop using fear as a readiness signal. It is not measuring whether you should proceed. It is measuring that something matters to you.

You are not waiting to become someone who is not afraid. That person does not arrive, and the people you assume are that person are simply further along in the practice of proceeding regardless.

Brave is not calm. Brave is scared, and doing it anyway.
