One Year From Now, You Will Wish You Had Started Today

02 September 2026

QPQuiet Progress02 September 2026 · 6 min read

6 min read

A year from now you will wish you had started today. This is not a threat, and it is not motivational pressure. It is simply the most reliably repeated observation people make about their own lives, and it is worth understanding why it keeps happening.

The reason is not laziness. It is that the person who benefits from starting today does not feel real to you yet.

Your future self is treated like a stranger

Hal Hershfield's research examines what he calls future self-continuity: how connected you feel to the person you will be years from now.

The finding that made the work widely known is a study published in 2011 in the Journal of Marketing Research, in which participants interacted with realistic, age-progressed renderings of their own future faces before making financial decisions. Those who saw the older version of themselves allocated substantially more money to long-term savings than those who did not.

Nothing about their finances changed. Nothing about the arguments for saving changed. What changed was that the beneficiary stopped being an abstraction and started being a recognisable person.

Related work found that people who report a stronger sense of overlap with their future selves are more willing to accept delayed rewards over immediate ones — the same disposition, showing up in laboratory decisions.

Delay is not really a discipline problem. It is a problem of who you think you are doing this for.

Why "one year" is the right unit

A year is long enough for the arithmetic to become impressive and short enough to remain real.

Twenty minutes a day is over a hundred and twenty hours. Two pages a week is a book-length manuscript. Three training sessions a week is roughly a hundred and fifty sessions — enough to change what your body can do, in any reasonable programme.

Ten years is too abstract to act on; ten days is too short to show anything. A year is the unit where consistent small inputs produce results that look, from the outside, like a different person.

And it passes regardless. That is the part people skip. The year is going to happen whether or not you use it, and this time next September something will exist or nothing will.

Today is not special, and that is the argument

The usual objection is that today is a bad day to start. You are tired, the week is busy, the conditions are wrong, and next month looks cleaner.

Next month will not be cleaner. It looks cleaner only because it is far enough away that its obligations have not been scheduled yet. This is a well-known feature of how people plan: distant periods appear open because their inevitable interruptions are not yet visible.

So the argument for today is not that today is a good day. It is that today is the only day that definitely exists, and the version of it available to you is not meaningfully worse than the version you are imagining for later.

There is a subtler cost to delay that the arithmetic misses. Waiting does not hold your position — it moves you backwards relative to where you would have been, and it also erodes the belief that you act on your intentions. Each postponed start makes the next one slightly less credible to you, so the price of a delayed year is paid twice: once in the work not done, and once in the confidence not built.

Making the person real

If the mechanism is that your future self feels like a stranger, then the useful interventions are the ones that make that person concrete.

Write to them, briefly and specifically. Not goals — a description. What their week looks like, what they can do that you cannot, what they are relieved about. Specific detail is what converts an abstraction into someone you would act on behalf of.

Then name the debt in both directions. Everything you are currently good at was paid for by an earlier version of you who did something inconvenient. You are the beneficiary of that person's ordinary Tuesdays. The obligation runs forward as well.

And keep the first action small enough to do today. Not the plan for the year — one action, in the next hour, that leaves evidence. The year is built from these, and the only one that matters right now is the first.

What to do with this

  • Write a paragraph about the person a year out. Concrete and specific. Read it when starting feels optional.
  • Do one small thing today. Twenty minutes, with something to show for it. Today's job is to make the start real, not to make it good.
  • Do the year's arithmetic. Multiply your daily unit by 365 and write the number down. It is usually larger than expected, and it makes the small unit feel worth defending.
  • Stop waiting for a clear month. The clear month is an artefact of distance. It never arrives, because it never existed.
  • Diarise the review. One year from today, look back. That entry is what turns a good intention into a measurable claim.

One year from now you will wish you had started today — not because today has any special properties, but because today is what you have, and because the year will pass either way.

Not because today is special. Because today is what you have. Use it.
