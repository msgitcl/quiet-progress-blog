You Are Allowed to Change Your Mind

02 September 2026

7 min read

There is a specific kind of stuck that does not look like laziness. You are working hard. You are showing up. You are just doing it on a road you privately stopped believing in some months ago, and you cannot work out why you are not allowed to leave.

The reason usually is not the future. It is the past — and specifically, how much of it you have already spent.

The theatre ticket study

In 1985 Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer ran an experiment at the Ohio University Theater. People arriving to buy a season ticket were, at random, given one of three prices: the full $15, a discounted $13, or a heavier discount at $8. Nobody knew the discount was coming, and nobody knew others were paying a different price. Everyone ended up holding an identical ticket to an identical season.

Then the researchers counted attendance.

The people who had paid full price attended significantly more plays in the first half of the season than the discounted groups — roughly four performances against about three and a bit. The only difference between those audiences was money that had already left their hands and could not come back.

That is the sunk cost effect in one clean picture. A cost you can no longer recover changed behaviour it should have had no bearing on. The value of a play on a Thursday night in November is the same regardless of what the ticket cost in September.

Why the mind does this

Economically it makes no sense. Psychologically it makes complete sense, and it is worth understanding rather than scolding yourself over.

Walking away requires you to admit, out loud and to yourself, that the earlier spending was wasted. Continuing lets you avoid that admission for a while longer. As long as the project is alive, the years are still "an investment." The moment you quit, they convert into a loss — and the mind will pay a great deal to postpone that conversion.

There is also identity. You told people about this. You have been the person doing this thing for three years. Quitting does not just end a project; it edits a story you have been telling about yourself, and there is no comfortable way to announce that edit.

The years are gone either way. Staying does not recover them. It only spends more of them keeping the loss out of view.

The question that actually decides it

There is one question that cuts through all of this, and it is uncomfortable precisely because it works.

Knowing everything I know now, and having spent nothing, would I start this today?

Not "have I put in too much to stop." Not "what will people think." Just: from here, with today's information, is this where the next three years should go?

If the answer is a clear yes, everything gets easier — the tiredness was tiredness, not doubt, and you can stop interrogating it. If the answer is no, then you have not just discovered you should quit. You have discovered that you decided to quit some time ago, and have been paying rent on the decision ever since.

Changing your mind is not the same as giving up

Our language is unhelpful here. We have one word — quitting — for two completely different acts.

One is abandoning something because it got hard, in the boring middle, before you had any real information. That one is usually a mistake, and it is the reason the advice "never quit" exists at all.

The other is stopping because you have new information: the work is not what you thought, you do not want the life it leads to, the market moved, the person you were when you chose it is not the person choosing now. That is not weakness. That is the entire function of learning. A person who cannot act on new information has not got discipline; they have got inertia with better branding.

Turning around on the wrong road is not going backwards. Every step you take after the turn is progress, and every step before it was not.

What you actually keep

The fear underneath all of this is that leaving makes the years meaningless. It does not, and this is not a consolation — it is a description of how skills work.

The specific thing may be dead. The capability is not. Three years of anything leaves you with judgement, tolerance for slow work, a working knowledge of how the thing gets made, contacts, taste, and an accurate sense of what you are like when a project gets difficult. All of that walks out of the door with you and shortens the next attempt.

People who change direction late are almost never starting from zero. They are starting from experience, which is a considerably better place to start from than where they began the first time.

What to do with this

  • Ask the fresh-start question in writing. "Spending nothing, knowing this, would I begin today?" Write the answer down. Written answers are harder to fudge than thought ones.
  • Separate the cost from the decision. List what you have spent. Then set that list aside completely — it is fixed, it is identical under both choices, and it should carry no weight in the comparison.
  • Set a review date instead of drifting. "I will give it until March, then decide properly." A deadline turns an endless private argument into a single scheduled decision.
  • Check whether it is doubt or difficulty. Difficulty says "this is hard and I still want it." Doubt says "even if this works, I do not want the outcome." Only the second is a reason to leave.
  • Name what transfers. Before you go, write down the ten things you know now that you did not know at the start. That list is what the years actually bought.

You are allowed to change your mind. Not on a bad Tuesday, not on impulse — but on evidence, deliberately, having asked the honest question and heard the honest answer.

Walking away from the wrong road is not failure. It is direction. Turn around, and start walking.
