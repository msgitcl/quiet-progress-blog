Consistency

It Is Built in the Hours Nobody Is Clapping For

02 September 2026

6 min read

The version of you that you are trying to become is not built in the moments you imagine. It is not built when the work is shown, praised or noticed. It is built in an empty room, on an ordinary evening, doing something nobody will ever see or comment on.

Those hours are the least satisfying part of the whole process, and they are the part that does the building.

What applause does to motivation

There is a classic experiment that explains why work done for an audience behaves so strangely.

In 1973, Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett studied nursery-school children who already enjoyed drawing. One group was told in advance they would receive a certificate for drawing, and got it. A second group received the same reward unexpectedly, after the fact. A third received nothing.

Some time later the researchers observed how much the children chose to draw during free play. The group that had expected a reward now drew noticeably less than the others.

The effect is known as overjustification. When an external reward is attached to something you were doing for its own sake, the activity can get quietly reclassified — from something you do because you want to, into something you do because it pays. Remove the payment and the motivation goes with it.

Work that runs on applause stops when the applause does. Work that runs without it does not have that failure mode.

The audience is not available at the useful times

Even setting the psychology aside, there is a scheduling problem: recognition arrives at the wrong times.

It comes at the end, if it comes at all, and it comes for outcomes. The work is at the beginning and in the middle, and it consists of process. If you need external response to keep going, then you need it precisely during the period when, structurally, there is none available — because you have not finished anything yet.

This is why so many attempts die at the same point. Not from difficulty, but from silence. The first burst of interest fades, nothing new arrives to replace it, and the work turns out to have been running on attention rather than on anything self-sustaining.

What actually happens in the empty room

It is worth being concrete about why unwitnessed hours matter more than witnessed ones, because it is not a moral point.

Improvement requires working at the edge of what you can currently do, which means being visibly bad at something for a while. That is difficult to do in front of people. Watched, you tend to perform what you already do well; alone, you can afford to fail repeatedly, which is the only condition under which skill actually moves.

Unwitnessed work also has no incentive to look impressive. Nobody is judging the middle of it, so you can spend an hour on the dull, structural, invisible part that a spectator would find boring and that determines whether the whole thing works.

And it builds something that no amount of external praise produces: the private knowledge that you do this whether or not anyone is watching. That is the material self-trust is made of, and it can only be acquired in the absence of an audience.

There is a further reason the unwitnessed hours matter, and it concerns what you end up making. Work produced with an audience in mind drifts towards what will be understood quickly and approved of, which is rarely the same as what is good. The interesting choices — the harder structure, the less obvious approach, the part that takes three months and cannot be explained in a sentence — almost always get made in private, because those are the only conditions in which nobody is available to be disappointed by them yet.

This is also why the middle of a project is the worst possible time to seek reactions. A half-built thing looks bad to everyone, including its maker. Show it too early and you will get accurate feedback on a version that was never meant to be judged, and you will usually respond by making it more ordinary.

Not a vow of silence

One qualification, because "work in silence" advice often overshoots.

Feedback is not the same as applause. You need real feedback — from the work itself, from people who know the field, from results — and cutting yourself off from it produces confident, unimproving people. What you want to reduce is dependence on response, not on information.

The practical distinction: seek feedback deliberately, at chosen points, on specific questions. Do not run your day-to-day motivation on whether anyone reacted to what you made this week.

What to do with this

  • Count sessions, not reactions. Your scoreboard should be made of things you control. Response is not one of them.
  • Do the invisible part on purpose. The structural, unglamorous work that nobody would applaud is usually the work that decides the outcome.
  • Separate feedback from applause. Ask specific people specific questions at specific times. That is information. Everything else is weather.
  • Notice when you are performing. If you are choosing what to work on based on what would look good being worked on, you have drifted from building to presenting.
  • Keep one thing entirely private. Something with no audience at all, purely to prove to yourself that the work does not require one.

The room is empty and nobody is clapping. That is not a sign that the work is unimportant. It is a description of where the work is done — and of the only conditions in which the person you are trying to become actually gets built.

Show up in the empty room. That is where it happens.
