Beat Yesterday by One Percent

02 September 2026

QPQuiet Progress02 September 2026 · 7 min read

7 min read

Most people set goals against a version of themselves that does not exist yet — the one who has already arrived. Then every day is measured against that person, and every day comes up short, because the gap between where you are and where you want to be is the one distance that never gets smaller on a daily view.

There is a smaller and far more useful scoreboard: the person you were yesterday.

The arithmetic, honestly

Start with the number everyone quotes. Improve by one percent a day for a year and you multiply by 1.01 three hundred and sixty-five times, which comes to about 37. Decline by one percent a day and you land at roughly a thirtieth of where you started. Same daily magnitude, wildly different destinations.

It is a striking piece of arithmetic and it deserves one honest caveat: it is arithmetic, not a law of nature. Nothing in real life compounds cleanly for 365 days. Skills plateau, bodies have ceilings, and a 1% gain in week one is a much bigger absolute thing than a 1% gain in month nine.

What the calculation gets right is the shape rather than the number. Small differences in daily direction produce large differences in outcome, and the effect is invisible at any point where you would think to check. On day thirty the two curves are still practically touching. All of the separation happens later, which is exactly why nobody believes it while it matters.

Where the idea was actually tested

The phrase people use for this is the aggregation of marginal gains, and it comes from Dave Brailsford, who took over as performance director of British Cycling in the early 2000s. British track cycling at that point had a thin record — a single Olympic gold in a very long time.

His method was not a breakthrough. It was a search for small percentages everywhere at once: bike seat ergonomics, tyres, hand-washing to reduce illness, pillow and mattress consistency so riders slept the same way in every hotel, the exact way team clothing was washed. None of these is a headline. Each was worth a fraction.

British Cycling went on to dominate the track at the 2008 Beijing Olympics, taking seven of the ten golds, and repeated it at London 2012. The same organisation's road team, Team Sky, produced a British winner of the Tour de France in 2012, the first in the race's history.

Nobody could point to the single decision that did it, which is the entire point. There was not one. There were hundreds of one-percents, held for years.

Why yesterday is the right comparison

Comparing yourself to a stranger tells you nothing you can act on. You do not know their starting point, their years, their resources, or how much of what you can see is real. The comparison produces a feeling and no instructions.

Comparing yourself to yesterday has the opposite properties. Same body, same constraints, same job, same amount of sleep available. It is the only comparison in your life with the variables held steady, which makes it the only one carrying real information.

It is also the only one you can definitely win. Yesterday's version of you is not an unreachable standard — it is a person you have complete data on, whose specific weak point you know, and who you only have to beat by a margin small enough to be almost embarrassing.

One percent is deliberately unimpressive

The size matters more than it looks. One percent is chosen precisely because it is too small to fail at.

Two more sentences than yesterday. One extra rep. Ten minutes earlier to bed. Reading one page before the phone. These are not inspiring targets, and that is their advantage: a target that only works when you feel good is not a target, it is a wish. A target this small survives a bad night, a heavy week, a flat mood.

There is also a quiet mechanical benefit. Very small increases rarely trigger the internal resistance that big changes do. Announce you are going to run five kilometres tomorrow and some part of you starts negotiating immediately. Announce you will run one minute longer than yesterday and there is nothing to argue with.

What this does not mean

It does not mean improving every single day. Some days you will be worse than yesterday, and the arithmetic does not collapse — human progress is not a smooth curve, it is a noisy line with an upward drift, and the drift is the only part that matters.

It also does not mean measuring everything. Tracking six variables is a hobby, not a method. Pick the one that most needs to move and let the rest sit still. One percent on one thing, held, beats one percent on ten things, abandoned in February.

What to do with this

  • Choose one measure. One number, one habit, one skill. If you cannot name it in a short sentence, it is too vague to improve.
  • Set the daily target absurdly low. If it does not feel slightly silly, it is too big to survive the week that goes wrong.
  • Ask the one-line question at night. "Was today marginally better than yesterday on that one thing?" Yes or no. No essay.
  • Look for the boring percentages. Sleep, a fixed start time, a tidied workspace, water, the thing you keep meaning to fix. Brailsford's list was mostly logistics, not talent.
  • Review monthly, not daily. Daily data is noise. Compare this month with the same month last year and you will finally see the slope.

Stop measuring yourself against strangers whose starting line you never saw. The only score that counts is the one you can actually verify, and it is kept against a person you know extremely well.

Beat yesterday's version of you by one percent. That is enough.
