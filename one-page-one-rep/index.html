One Page. One Rep. One Honest Hour.

02 September 2026

6 min read

One page. One rep. One honest hour. Held up against the size of what you want, it looks like nothing — so small that doing it feels almost like a way of avoiding the real work.

It looks like nothing today. In a year it looks like the only thing that ever worked.

Why small units are not a compromise

In 1984 the organisational psychologist Karl Weick published a paper in American Psychologist called "Small Wins," arguing something that sounds modest and is not: large problems are frequently unsolvable as stated.

His point was about scale and arousal. When a problem is framed as enormous — end poverty, transform your health, become a writer — the framing itself raises the stakes so high that thinking degrades. People become anxious, options narrow, and the most common response to a problem defined that large is paralysis, not action.

Weick's alternative was to recast the large problem as a series of small, concrete, controllable wins. A small win is a real outcome of modest size. It is achievable, it is visible, and — this is the part that matters — each one changes the situation slightly, revealing the next move.

Small wins are not a scaled-down version of the ambition. They are the only form in which the ambition is actually workable, because they are sized to fit the attention and control a person actually has.

A big problem is not solved by a big effort. It is dismantled by a sequence of small ones, each of which shows you where the next one goes.

The arithmetic is not subtle

Take the units seriously for a moment.

One page a day is roughly 365 pages a year — considerably more than most published books contain. One extra rep per session, three sessions a week, is a hundred and fifty-six additional reps you would not otherwise have done. One honest hour a day, five days a week, is two hundred and sixty hours a year: more than six full working weeks devoted to a single thing.

None of that requires an unusual amount of discipline on any given day. It requires that the day happens repeatedly, which is a different property entirely — and the reason people miss it is that the daily unit is genuinely unimpressive. You cannot feel two hundred and sixty hours. You can only feel the one in front of you, and that one always feels like too little to matter.

The honest hour

One qualifier does the heavy lifting in all of this: honest.

An honest hour is an hour actually spent on the difficult centre of the work, not on the pleasant edges of it. Reading about the thing is not the thing. Reorganising your notes is not the thing. Choosing tools, watching tutorials, planning the schedule — all of that feels like work and generates no small wins, because nothing has been produced or attempted.

This distinction explains a common and painful situation: people who put in a great deal of time and have very little to show for it. The hours were real. They were spent adjacent to the work rather than inside it.

Sixty honest minutes beats four dishonest hours, and it is not close. If your hours are not producing small wins, the problem is usually not the quantity.

Why the small unit survives

There is a further, practical reason to shrink the unit, separate from the arithmetic: a small unit survives contact with real life.

A plan built around three-hour deep-work blocks fails on the day the boiler breaks. A plan built around one page does not. And because it does not fail, you never enter the state where the plan is broken and therefore abandonable — which is how nearly every ambitious plan actually ends.

Small units also do something to how you see yourself. Every completed unit is a small piece of evidence that you do what you say. Accumulate a few hundred and you are not a person trying to build a habit; you are a person who does this. That shift is worth more than any single session, and it is only available to people whose daily requirement is small enough to keep meeting.

What to do with this

  • Name the unit precisely. "One page," "one set," "one call." Not "work on it," which cannot be completed and therefore cannot be counted.
  • Size it for your worst day. The unit should be doable on a bad day with a headache. On good days you will exceed it, and that is fine — the floor is what carries the year.
  • Count units, not hours. Hours reward presence. Units reward production, and only production compounds.
  • Ask the honesty question. At the end of the hour: what exists now that did not exist before? If the answer is nothing, the hour was adjacent to the work.
  • Look at the year, not the day. Any single unit is too small to be encouraging. Twelve months of them is not, and that view is the only one that shows the truth.

The reason this advice keeps being given is not that it is inspiring. It is that the alternative — waiting for a stretch of time large enough to feel proportionate to the goal — describes a stretch of time that almost never arrives.

One page. One rep. One honest hour. It looks like nothing today. It looks like everything in a year.
