No Storm Has Ever Stayed Forever

02 September 2026

QPQuiet Progress02 September 2026 · 6 min read

6 min read

This week feels heavy. Not dramatic, not a crisis — just weighted, in a way that makes ordinary things take more effort than they should and makes the future look like a longer version of right now.

That last part is the mistake, and it is not a personal failing. It is a specific, well-documented error that human beings make about their own feelings.

You are bad at predicting how long this lasts

Psychologists call it affective forecasting: predicting how you will feel, and for how long, after some future event. Daniel Gilbert, Timothy Wilson and colleagues spent years measuring these predictions against what actually happened.

The findings are consistent. People are reasonably good at predicting the direction of a feeling — you correctly know that a rejection will feel bad and a success will feel good. What they get wrong is intensity and duration. Bad events are predicted to hurt harder and much longer than they do. This is called the impact bias, and its duration half is sometimes called the durability bias.

The explanation Gilbert and Wilson offered is that we have a psychological immune system: a set of largely unconscious processes that rationalise, reframe, find silver linings, and generally metabolise bad events. It is remarkably effective, and — crucially — it is invisible to us when we forecast. So we imagine the bad thing landing on the person we are today, permanently, with no recovery machinery attached.

You are not predicting the future. You are extending the present in a straight line, using a model of yourself that leaves out the part that heals.

Why heavy weeks feel permanent

There is a second mechanism stacked on top, and anyone who has had a bad month recognises it: mood colours memory and expectation.

In a low state, past difficulties come to mind more readily than past good stretches, and future plans get evaluated through the same filter. The result is a very convincing internal case that this is simply what your life is like — assembled from real evidence, selectively retrieved.

This is why the thought "it has always been like this and it always will be" arrives with such authority in week three of something hard. It is not a considered judgement. It is a mood doing arithmetic on a biased sample.

The practical consequence: do not decide anything permanent

If your forecast is systematically wrong in one direction, then decisions made from inside a heavy week are systematically wrong in that same direction.

This is the actual, usable point. Not "cheer up." Simply: a temporary state is a poor place from which to make permanent decisions. Quitting the project, ending the relationship, abandoning the plan, writing off the year — these may all be correct decisions, and none of them are improved by being made on Thursday of a bad week.

The heavy state is not lying to you about how you feel. It is lying to you about how long you are going to feel it, and it is that second number that most big decisions depend on.

You do not have to fix it today

The instinct in a bad stretch is to fix it — to solve the mood, work out what is wrong, restructure something. And sometimes there is a real problem with a real solution, in which case, good.

Often there is not. Often the honest description is that this is a hard patch, it has causes you already know about, and it will pass on a schedule you cannot accelerate by thinking about it harder. In that case the demand to fix it becomes a second problem sitting on top of the first: now you are having a difficult week and failing to solve it.

The alternative is unglamorous. You do not have to fix it today. You have to stay — keep the basic structure running, keep the smallest version of your commitments, and let the weather do what weather does.

Staying is not passivity. It is the recognition that some things resolve with time and cannot be resolved without it, and that your only real job during those stretches is to still be standing when the conditions change.

What to do with this

  • Postpone permanent decisions by two weeks. Write the decision down, date it, and revisit. If it is right, it will still be right in a fortnight, and you will be reading it in different weather.
  • Shrink the commitments, do not cancel them. The smallest version keeps the structure alive. Cancelling entirely means rebuilding from nothing later, which is much harder than it sounds.
  • Check the basics before the meaning. Sleep, food, daylight, movement, and how long since you spoke to someone. A surprising share of existential weight is downstream of those five, and none of them require insight.
  • Write down the last one. The previous heavy stretch, when it started, when it lifted. Your own history is the best available evidence against "this is permanent," and in the moment you will not recall it unaided.
  • Say the honest version, not the cheerful one. "This is hard and it will pass" is both true and bearable. "Everything is fine" is neither, and it costs energy to maintain.

No storm in recorded history has stayed forever. Not one. The sky always clears, and the fact that you cannot currently picture it clearing is a known property of the state you are in — not information about the future.

You do not have to fix it today. You only have to stay.
