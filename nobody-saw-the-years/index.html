Everyone Saw the Result. Nobody Saw the Years.

02 September 2026

6 min read

Everyone saw the result. The finished book, the healthy body, the business that works, the person who looks like they have it together. It arrived in the world fully formed, on a Tuesday, and it looked effortless because you only met it after it was finished.

Nobody saw the years. Not the early mornings, not the doubt, not the many days it was nearly abandoned. And because nobody saw them, everybody quietly assumes they were not there.

The planes that came back

During the Second World War, the American military had a problem: bombers were being lost, and armour was heavy. You could not armour the whole aircraft, so where should the plating go?

The obvious approach was to examine the aircraft returning from missions and map where they had been hit. The damage clustered on the wings, the fuselage, the tail. The recommendation nearly wrote itself: reinforce the places with the most bullet holes.

Abraham Wald, a statistician working with the Statistical Research Group in New York, pointed out that the data set was fatally incomplete. Those were the planes that came back. The distribution of damage on returning aircraft did not show where bombers were most often hit — it showed where a bomber could be hit and still make it home.

The armour, he argued, belonged where the returning planes showed no damage at all: the engines, the cockpit. The planes hit there were not in the sample, because they never landed.

You are studying the survivors and drawing conclusions about the whole population. The most important part of the data set is the part you cannot see.

Everything you admire is a returning plane

Every success story you have ever encountered reached you because it succeeded. That is not a coincidence, it is the selection criterion. Nobody writes a profile of the person who tried the same thing with the same intelligence and had it not work.

This distorts two things at once.

It distorts method: you learn what winners did, without knowing whether the same behaviour also appears in everyone who lost. Dropping out of university looks decisive in a founder's biography; you never see the far larger group who dropped out and are not being interviewed about it.

And it distorts timeline, which is the part that hurts on a personal level. The story is always compressed. Ten years become a paragraph. The two years of nothing become "then things started to come together." You are comparing your slow, unedited middle to a summary written after the fact, by someone with the benefit of knowing how it ended.

The years exist. They are just not filmable.

Here is what the summary leaves out, in nearly every case: a long stretch of unglamorous repetition with no external feedback, during which the person had no idea whether it was working.

That period is not omitted out of modesty. It is omitted because it is genuinely boring. There is nothing to photograph in the eleventh month of a routine. There is no moment in it, no turning point, no scene. It is just a person doing an ordinary thing on an ordinary day, again.

But that stretch is where the entire thing was actually built. The visible result is the last one percent — the part that finally became presentable. The years were the other ninety-nine, and they looked exactly like what you are doing now, which is precisely why they were left out.

You saw yours

Now turn it around, because this is the part that matters.

You have full access to one data set that nobody else can see: your own. You know every early morning, every quiet doubt, every day you almost stopped and did not. From the inside it looks like a mess, because you are seeing the whole distribution rather than the summary.

You are not judging your work against their work. You are judging your process against their product, and no process has ever looked good next to a finished thing.

Two consequences follow. First, your standard for yourself is unfair by construction, and adjusting it is not lowering the bar — it is correcting a measurement error. Second, the thing you are inclined to be embarrassed about — that it has taken this long, that it has been this uneven — is the ordinary texture of every result you have ever admired. You are simply seeing it without the edit.

What to do with this

  • Ask where the missing planes are. When someone's method impresses you, ask how many people did the same thing and did not make it. The answer changes what you should copy.
  • Restore the timeline. Find out how long the person had actually been working before the moment you first heard of them. It is nearly always years longer than the story implies.
  • Compare like with like. Your rough draft against their rough draft; your year three against their year three. Never your process against their product.
  • Keep the record you are already living. A dated log makes your invisible years visible later, to you, when you need the evidence most.
  • Expect the summary treatment. If this works, people will describe your decade in one sentence too, and it will be just as misleading. That is not unfair — it is what results look like from outside.

Nobody saw the years. That is not a sign that yours are not counting. It is a description of how results are always presented, and of why the middle of anything feels so much lonelier than it should.

You saw those years. And you kept going.
