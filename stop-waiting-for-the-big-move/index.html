Stop Waiting for the Big Move

02 September 2026

6 min read

Somewhere in the back of most people's plans there is a big move: the year off, the relocation, the funding, the clean six-month stretch with nothing else in it. Real progress begins then. Everything before it is preparation, and everything you could do this week is too small to bother with.

The big move is usually not coming, and it is not what would have made the difference anyway.

Why the small thing gets dismissed

Ten minutes looks pointless because you are comparing it to the goal instead of to the alternative.

Against "become fluent in a language," ten minutes is nothing. Against the actual alternative — zero minutes, which is what happens on the days you decide it is not worth starting — it is the entire difference between a thing that exists and a thing that does not.

Run the numbers and the dismissal looks worse. Ten minutes a day is a little over sixty hours in a year. One page a day is a book-length manuscript. Two hundred words a day is enough for a substantial piece of work annually. None of these requires a free month; they require a normal life with one small commitment inside it.

What makes the small unit hard to respect is that it produces no visible change today, and today is the only frame in which you ever evaluate it.

The big move is usually a consequence

Here is the part that reverses the logic entirely.

Look closely at other people's big moves and most of them turn out to be the result of an accumulation, not the start of one. The person who took six months off had savings and a skill that made the leap survivable. The career change happened because of a side project that had existed for two years. The dramatic public moment was preceded by a long private one.

The big move is rarely the cause of the progress. It is the point where accumulated progress became large enough to justify a visible change — and it looks like a starting gun only from outside.

Waiting for the big move to begin is waiting for the effect to arrive before the cause.

Small does not mean vague

There is a failure mode here that has to be named, because "start small" advice usually stops one step too early and then does not work.

A small intention with no specifics is just a wish. "I will read more" and "I will do ten minutes when I can" both fail, and they fail for the same reason: nothing tells you when the behaviour is supposed to start.

The fix is well studied. Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran published a meta-analysis in 2006 of what are called implementation intentions — plans in the form if situation Y occurs, then I will do X, spelling out when, where and how in advance. Across 94 independent tests, forming one produced a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, around d = .65.

That is a substantial improvement, obtained not from more motivation or a bigger goal, but from adding a time, a place and a trigger to an intention that already existed.

So the instruction is not "do something small." It is: do this specific small thing, at this time, in this place, after this cue. The difference between those two sentences accounts for an enormous amount of the difference between people who keep going and people who do not.

There is a second reason the specificity matters, and it is about what happens on the days you do not want to. A vague intention has to be re-argued every time it comes up, and you will lose that argument roughly half the time. A cued plan has already answered the question of when, so the only thing left is the doing. Over a year that difference is not marginal — it is most of the sessions.

Small units are also more accurate

One further advantage, which matters more the less you know about the thing you are attempting.

A big move is a large bet placed at the moment of maximum ignorance — before you have found out whether you like the work, whether you are any good at it, or whether the plan survives contact with reality. Plenty of people have taken the year off and discovered in month two that they did not want the thing they were making room for.

Small units give you the same information at a fraction of the cost. Ten minutes a day for a month tells you whether you will still be doing this in a year, and it tells you before you have staked anything you cannot afford to lose.

What to do with this

  • Name the smallest real unit. One page, ten minutes, two hundred words. It must be completable on your worst day.
  • Attach a when, a where and a cue. "After I make coffee, at the kitchen table, ten minutes." That specificity is what the research is measuring.
  • Compare it to zero, not to the goal. Ten minutes versus nothing is the honest comparison, and it is the one you are actually choosing between.
  • Treat the big move as a possible later consequence. Not a prerequisite. If it comes, it will come out of the accumulation, not before it.
  • Start the unit this week. Not after the current busy period. The busy period is not an interruption to normal life; over a long enough view, it is normal life.

Small does not mean pointless. It means sized to a real week, which is the only kind of week that ever actually arrives.

Stop waiting for the big move. Make the small one.
