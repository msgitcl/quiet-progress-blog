You Are Not Starting From Zero. You Are Starting From Experience.

02 September 2026

6 min read

Starting again feels like starting from nothing. The old thing ended, the plan did not work, the years appear to have produced no usable result — and now you are back at the beginning, except older, which seems worse than the beginning.

You are not at zero. You are at the beginning of a different thing, carrying a set of assets you did not have the first time and have not counted.

Almost nothing is domain-specific

Look closely at what a few years of serious effort actually leaves behind, and very little of it is tied to the specific project that ended.

You know how long things take, which is a genuinely rare skill and one that almost nobody has at the start. You know what you are like when work becomes difficult — where you avoid, what you tell yourself, which weeks are dangerous. You know how to work with people, how to finish, how to ship something imperfect, how to recover after a bad month. You have judgement about quality that you did not have before, because judgement is built by having made things and seen what was wrong with them.

The specific knowledge dies with the project. Everything above travels, and it is most of what separates a second attempt from a first one.

The project ended. The capability did not. Those are two different accounts, and only one of them was closed.

What difficulty leaves behind

There is a research literature on this, and it deserves to be reported carefully rather than triumphantly.

Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun developed the concept of post-traumatic growth: positive psychological changes that some people report after struggling with a major crisis. Their inventory groups them into five areas — a greater sense of personal strength, changed relationships with others, an openness to new possibilities, a deeper appreciation of life, and for some, spiritual change.

Two honest caveats. This is what people report, and researchers continue to debate how well self-reported growth maps onto measured change. And growth is not automatic, not universal, and emphatically not a reason to seek out hardship — plenty of difficulty leaves nothing but damage.

What survives the caveats is modest and still useful: it is common for people who have come through something hard to find they carry a more accurate sense of what they can handle. Not a gift, not a silver lining. Simply the ordinary result of having been tested and having data about it.

The second attempt is a different attempt

The reason people underestimate all of this is that they compare the wrong things. They compare their current emotional state — flat, tired, disillusioned — to the enthusiasm they had at the original start, and conclude they are worse off.

But enthusiasm was never the asset. It was the fuel that got the first attempt off the ground, and it is not what makes the second one work.

What makes the second one work is accuracy. You will size the plan more realistically. You will recognise the warning signs earlier. You will waste less time on the things that turned out not to matter. You will move faster through the parts you have already done once, and you will know sooner when something is going wrong.

That is why second attempts are frequently much shorter than first ones. It is not that the person became more talented. They stopped paying tuition on the same lessons.

There is one asset people miss entirely, because it does not feel like one: you now know what you do not want. Ruling something out is real information, and it is expensive information — most people never acquire it because they never commit hard enough to find out. Knowing, from evidence rather than imagination, that a particular kind of work or life does not suit you removes an entire branch from every future decision.

Extracting it deliberately

The transfer is not automatic. Left alone, a failed attempt tends to leave a vague residue — an impression that it was awful and you would rather not repeat it — which is close to useless.

Making it usable requires converting the experience into specifics, in writing, at least once. What actually went wrong, in order. What you would do differently. What you now know about how you behave under pressure. What you are better at than you were.

That document is the thing you actually take with you. Without it, the years become a feeling. With it, they become a method.

What to do with this

  • Write the ten-item list. Ten things you know now that you did not know before. It will take an hour and it will be longer than you expect.
  • Separate the project from the capability. Name what ended, and name what you still have. Most people conflate the two and mourn both.
  • Expect flatness, not enthusiasm. The second start does not feel like the first. That is not a bad sign — it is what accuracy feels like from inside.
  • Set the new timeline from real data. You now have actual evidence about how long things take. Use it instead of optimism.
  • Do not wait to feel recovered. Some of the recovery happens through working on something new, and cannot be completed beforehand.

Everything that broke taught you something specific, and specific things are what you build the next attempt out of. You have already paid for them. The only remaining question is whether you collect.

You are not starting from zero. You are starting from experience. Use it, and go again.
